## Q1. Singh & Fleming (2010): do lone inventors produce more or fewer breakthroughs, and more or fewer failures, than teams?

### Takeaway
Lone inventors produce **fewer breakthroughs and more very poor outcomes**. The study covers 500,000+ patents, and lone inventors (especially those with no organizational affiliation) do worse at both tails. Working alone therefore does not buy "high variance with more home runs." In the working-paper version, four-person teams are roughly 64–65% more likely than lone inventors to reach the top 5% or top 1% of citations. Part of the team advantage is statistically explained by team members' diverse technical experience and the size of their external networks. The evidence is correlational.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Jasjit Singh & Lee Fleming, "Lone Inventors as Sources of Breakthroughs: Myth or Reality?", *Management Science* 56(1): 41–56. Issue dated January 2010; some sources give 2009 because it appeared online first. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1090.1072. — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.1090.1072); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/inmormnsc/v_3a56_3ay_3a2010_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a41-56.htm); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1299064)
- **Evidence type:** observational regression analysis of patent records, including quantile regressions and a mediation analysis. Correlational, not causal. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/inmormnsc/v_3a56_3ay_3a2010_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a41-56.htm)
- **[Abstract] Theory:** Prior work "considered the variance of creative outcome distributions, implicitly assuming a symmetric thickening or thinning of both tails." The authors argue instead that collaboration "reduces the probability of very poor outcomes—due to more rigorous selection processes—while simultaneously increasing the probability of extremely successful outcomes—due to greater recombinant opportunity in creative search." — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/inmormnsc/v_3a56_3ay_3a2010_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a41-56.htm); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1299064)
- **[Abstract] Main result:** "Analysis of over half a million patented inventions supports these arguments: individuals working alone, especially those without affiliation to organizations, are less likely to achieve breakthroughs and more likely to invent particularly poor outcomes." — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/inmormnsc/v_3a56_3ay_3a2010_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a41-56.htm); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1299064)
- **[Abstract] Not just a mean shift:** "Quantile regressions demonstrate that the effect is more than an upward mean shift." — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/inmormnsc/v_3a56_3ay_3a2010_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a41-56.htm)
- **[Abstract] Proposed mechanisms (partial mediation):** "We find partial mediation of the effect of collaboration on extreme outcomes by the diversity of technical experience of team members and by the size of team members' external collaboration networks." — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/inmormnsc/v_3a56_3ay_3a2010_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a41-56.htm); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1299064)
- **[Full text, WP] Breakthrough definition:** a breakthrough is a patent in the top 1% by citation frequency among patents with the same application year and technology class. A parallel top-5% indicator is also used. — [INSEAD author PDF](https://www.insead.edu/sites/insead/files/assets/faculty-personal-site/jasjit-singh/documents/singh_fleming_lone-inventors.pdf); [INSEAD WP 2008/69](https://flora.insead.edu/fichiersti_wp/inseadwp2008/2008-69.pdf)
- **[Full text, WP] Effect sizes** — [INSEAD author PDF](https://www.insead.edu/sites/insead/files/assets/faculty-personal-site/jasjit-singh/documents/singh_fleming_lone-inventors.pdf); [INSEAD WP 2009/31](https://flora.insead.edu/fichiersti_wp/inseadwp2009/2009-31.pdf); [INSEAD WP 2008/69](https://flora.insead.edu/fichiersti_wp/inseadwp2008/2008-69.pdf):
  - Four-person teams were about **64% more likely (top 5%)** and **65% more likely (top 1%)** than lone inventors to produce a breakthrough patent.
  - Patents assigned to organizations were **63% more likely** than unassigned patents to reach the 95th citation percentile. (So "affiliation" appears to be measured by patent assignment; this is inferred from the wording.)
  - Team plus organizational affiliation was associated with **2.11 times** the breakthrough likelihood of an unaffiliated lone inventor.
  - The citation benefit appears to rise steadily with team size.
- **[Full text, WP] Descriptive distribution** — [INSEAD author PDF](https://www.insead.edu/sites/insead/files/assets/faculty-personal-site/jasjit-singh/documents/singh_fleming_lone-inventors.pdf):
  - The "individual inventor" row of the working paper's percentile table lists 260,438 patents.
  - The 95th-percentile citation count is **31 for lone inventors vs 47 for four-person teams**.
  - For every team size, the 5th percentile is 0 citations and the 10th is 1.
  - The lone-inventor median is 6.
  - So zero-citation patents are common for everyone. The team advantage shows mainly in the upper tail and in fewer low-impact outcomes.
- **Later qualification:** Chan, Mihm & Sosa (2020) find the lone-inventor penalty holds for utility patents but not for design patents (see Q6). — [INSEAD publication page](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/revisiting-role-collaboration-creating-breakthrough); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2962348)

### Inferences
- **Against the "lone genius" story.** The user expects solo work to be a lottery ticket with more big wins. This is the strongest contrary evidence found. Across technology fields, working alone goes with a *thinner* top tail *and* a *fatter* bottom tail, not a symmetric widening.
- **Working alone is not the same as being broad.** The study compares team size, not specialist vs generalist individuals. But the mediators (team members' diverse technical experience and their external networks) suggest part of the team advantage comes from breadth of experience and outside ties. A solo creator might recapture *some* of that advantage, though not all, since the mediation is only partial.
- **What the two mechanisms mean in practice (inference; not tested in the paper):**
  - *Selection:* teams kill bad ideas more rigorously. A solo developer's substitute is structured outside critique: playtests, peer review, public demos.
  - *Recombination:* teams bring more ideas. The substitute is a deliberately diverse input diet plus an active external network.
- **The unaffiliated solo case is the worst.** For an indie developer, a publisher, incubator, developer community or mentor network may play the role of the "organization" (inference).
- **Limitations (inferred; not stated in the retrieved text):**
  - Selection bias: people who work alone and unaffiliated may differ in resources, skill or ambition, so the gap is not purely a team effect.
  - Citations measure technical influence, not commercial or artistic success.
  - Patents are not creative content products.
  - The working-paper numbers may differ from the published tables.
  - Relative effects such as +64% sit on low base rates (top 5% and top 1% by construction), so the absolute differences are small.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved:
  - the definition of "particularly poor outcomes" (zero citations? a bottom percentile?) and the size of the bottom-tail effect;
  - the share of the collaboration effect mediated by diversity and by networks;
  - the published (rather than working-paper) coefficients;
  - the sample years and patent office.
- Matt Clancy's synthesis "Highly cited innovation takes a team" surfaced in search but could not be fetched — [New Things Under the Sun](https://www.newthingsunderthesun.com/pub/adgcvkad/release/6)

## Q2. Boh, Evaristo & Ouderkirk (2014): how did specialists, generalists and "polymaths" compare at 3M?

### Takeaway
At 3M:
- **Breadth** went with producing *many* inventions, but not necessarily *technically influential* ones.
- **Depth** went with technically influential inventions.
- Only inventors with **both breadth and depth** were deemed "highly valuable," a judgment tied to turning inventions into commercial products.

The paper adds "polymaths" (high breadth plus high depth) to the usual specialist/generalist split. The evidence is correlational and from a single firm, and effect sizes could not be retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Boh, Evaristo & Ouderkirk, "Balancing breadth and depth of expertise for innovation: A 3M story," *Research Policy* 43(2): 349–366 (March 2014; online 2013), DOI 10.1016/j.respol.2013.10.009. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/respol/v43y2014i2p349-366.html); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733313001881); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259095529_Balancing_breadth_and_depth_of_expertise_for_innovation_A_3M_story)
- **Evidence type:** a single-firm empirical study of inventors. Correlational. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Balancing-breadth-and-depth-of-expertise-for-A-3M-Boh-Evaristo/73f61a4bee9a8a98a1cd73ee1f9502b93ed76ff5)
- **[Abstract] Scope:** the study "examines how inventors' breadth and depth of expertise influence innovation in 3M, a company renowned for sustained innovation for over a century." — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Balancing-breadth-and-depth-of-expertise-for-A-3M-Boh-Evaristo/73f61a4bee9a8a98a1cd73ee1f9502b93ed76ff5); [Mendeley](https://www.mendeley.com/catalogue/balancing-breadth-depth-expertise-innovation-3m-story)
- **[Abstract] Key results:** "breadth of inventor expertise relates to the generation of many inventions, but not necessarily to those that are technically influential. Depth of inventor expertise enables individuals to generate technically influential inventions… both breadth and depth of expertise are required for innovators to be deemed highly valuable." — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Balancing-breadth-and-depth-of-expertise-for-A-3M-Boh-Evaristo/73f61a4bee9a8a98a1cd73ee1f9502b93ed76ff5); [Mendeley](https://www.mendeley.com/catalogue/balancing-breadth-depth-expertise-innovation-3m-story); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733313001881)
- **[Search-extracted from journal page] What "highly valuable" means:** it concerns converting inventions into commercially successful products. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733313001881); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259095529_Balancing_breadth_and_depth_of_expertise_for_innovation_A_3M_story)
- **[Search-extracted from journal page] Typology:**
  - Prior research classifies individuals as specialists ("high depth and low breadth of expertise") or generalists ("high breadth and low depth of expertise").
  - The paper identifies a further group of 3M inventors with high breadth *and* high depth, which it calls polymaths.
  - — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733313001881); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259095529_Balancing_breadth_and_depth_of_expertise_for_innovation_A_3M_story)
- **(secondary) How citing papers describe it** — [Frontiers in Research Metrics & Analytics (2020)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/research-metrics-and-analytics/articles/10.3389/frma.2020.575862/full):
  - Boh et al. classify enterprise researchers into three groups (specialists, generalists and polymaths) by breadth or depth of expertise.
  - Another summary describes polymaths as having breadth plus *at least one* area of depth.
  - In one citing account: specialists worked on difficult technical subjects; generalists added value by integrating domains; polymaths held patents in their depth area and, because of their broad knowledge, did this better than generalists.
- **Insider co-author:** Andrew J. Ouderkirk is a senior 3M scientist. He was elected to the US National Academy of Engineering in 2005 for developing and commercializing multilayer polymer films with unique optical properties. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_J._Ouderkirk). (secondary) A 2020 citing article credits him with 308 patents as of September 2020. — [Frontiers (2020)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/research-metrics-and-analytics/articles/10.3389/frma.2020.575862/full)

### Inferences
- **The closest direct test of a T- (or π-) shaped profile found.** Breadth alone buys *volume* without *influence*; depth buys technical influence; commercial value at the firm required both. This contradicts "only specialists succeed," and equally "breadth alone is enough."
- **Applied to the user (inference from the pattern, not the study's test):**
  - At least one leg should be genuinely deep. For a 3D character-animation game, the natural candidate is character animation or character art.
  - The other roles (modeling, music, AI-assisted code) need only reach "competent enough to integrate."
- **The definition matters.** The brief describes polymaths as "deep in more than one area," but the retrieved sources do not say that. They describe *high breadth plus high depth*, and one citing paper says "at least one area of depth." The final report should not claim 3M polymaths were deep in several fields unless the full text confirms it.
- **Limitations (inferred):**
  - One company, which the paper itself calls exceptionally innovative, so generalizability is unclear.
  - Breadth and depth were probably measured from patent records (unconfirmed).
  - Correlational, so able people may simply become both deep and broad.
  - The "highly valuable" judgment may reflect internal evaluation.
  - An insider co-author gives access but may shape the framing.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: sample size, how breadth and depth were operationalized, how "highly valuable" was measured, and all effect sizes.
- I searched "Carlton Society" as a possible value measure; no link was confirmed.
- No independent replication of the three-way typology outside 3M was found.

## Q3. Liu, Dehmamy, Chown, Giles & Wang (2021): what sequence of exploration and exploitation precedes hot streaks?

### Takeaway
Hot streaks follow **exploration, then exploitation**: varied styles or topics first, then a focused period. Exploitation alone is associated with significantly *lower*-than-expected odds of a hot streak, and exploration alone also with lower odds. Compared with a baseline, the sequence raises the chance of a hot streak by **20.5% for artists, 13.8% for film directors and 19.2% for scientists**. The evidence is observational.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Liu, Dehmamy, Chown, Giles & Wang, "Understanding the onset of hot streaks across artistic, cultural, and scientific careers," *Nature Communications* 12: 5392 (online 13 Sep 2021), DOI 10.1038/s41467-021-25477-8; preprint arXiv 2103.01256. — [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-25477-8); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8438033); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01256); [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/understanding-the-onset-of-hot-streaks-across-artistic-cultural-a/)
- **Evidence type:** observational analysis of career histories, using deep-learning and network-science representations of creative outputs. Correlational. — [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-25477-8)
- **[Abstract] Motivation:** "Despite their ubiquitous nature across a wide range of creative domains, it remains unclear if there is any regularity underlying the beginning of successful periods in a career." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01256); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8438033)
- **[Abstract] Main finding:**
  - Hot streaks are "associated with neither exploration nor exploitation behavior in isolation, but a particular sequence of exploration followed by exploitation."
  - The shift from exploration to exploitation closely tracks when the hot streak begins.
  - Before a streak, people explore diverse styles or topics; once it begins, they become notably more focused.
  - — [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-25477-8); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8438033); [Kellogg Insight](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/artists-scientists-career-hot-streak)
- **[Full text, arXiv] Effect sizes** — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.01256):
  - Exploration followed by exploitation raises the probability of a hot streak over baseline by **20.5% for artists, 13.8% for directors and 19.2% for scientists**. (secondary) Press restates the comparison as "versus after a random point in a given career." — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2021/09/210913135720.htm)
  - Exploitation *not* preceded by exploration coincides with a hot streak significantly less often than expected.
  - Exploration *not* followed by exploitation also makes a hot streak less likely.
- **Outcome measures:**
  - artists: auction prices;
  - directors: IMDb ratings;
  - scientists: citations within 10 years.
  - — search-extracted from [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.01256) and [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8438033)
- **(secondary) Sample:** 2,128 artists, 4,337 film directors and 20,040 scientists. The art data are 800,000+ images from museum and gallery collections. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2021/09/210913135720.htm); [UPI](https://www.upi.com/Science_News/2021/09/13/creative-hot-streak/5571631537925/)
  - Caveat: a search summary of the 2018 precursor gives the same 4,337 directors (about 79,000 films) and 20,040 scientists, so the two papers may share datasets.
- **Precursor:** Liu et al. (2018), "Hot streaks in artistic, cultural, and scientific careers," *Nature* 559: 396–399. It found hot streaks across all three domains, with timing within a career that looks random and without an accompanying change in productivity. — [Northwestern Scholars](https://www.scholars.northwestern.edu/en/publications/hot-streaks-in-artistic-cultural-and-scientific-careers); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29995850/)
- **(secondary) Origin of the idea:** Dashun Wang traced the hypothesis to noticing that Van Gogh's paintings narrowed in style and subject after he moved to southern France. — [Kellogg Insight](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/artists-scientists-career-hot-streak); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-athletes-way/202109/how-van-goghs-hot-streak-fits-some-universal-patterns)

### Inferences
- **A time-ordered T: breadth first, then depth.** Peaks follow wide exploration and then commitment to a narrow vein. Staying broad forever, or narrowing without exploring first, both do worse.
- **Important scope caveat.** "Exploration" here means variety of *style or topic within one craft*: an artist's visual styles, a director's film content, a scientist's research topics. It is **not** evidence about holding several *job roles* (modeler + animator + composer). It supports "explore your game's art style and mechanics, then commit." It does not show that doing many roles is good.
- **Modest effects.** Lifts of about 14–21% on a baseline probability are real but modest; hot streaks remain hard to predict.
- **Limitations (inferred):**
  - Observational, so causation could run the other way: early success may cause focus.
  - Survivorship: the careers studied are long and visible enough to appear in auction, IMDb and citation records.
  - Success proxies are domain-specific (an auction price is not artistic merit).
  - The exploration and exploitation measures depend on the embedding models used.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: the exact thresholds for classifying exploration vs exploitation, confidence intervals, and the 2021 paper's own sample table. The sample sizes above are secondary.

## Q4. Dahlin, Taylor & Fichman (2004): are independent inventors over-represented at both the best and worst tails?

### Takeaway
**Yes, in a small single-industry sample.** Among 225 US tennis-racket patents (1981–1991), independent inventors' inventions were over-represented among **both** the most and the least impactful. Independents are a mixed group of "heroes" and "hobbyists," and their commercial success is reportedly low (secondary). This contrasts with Singh & Fleming's much larger cross-field sample, where lone inventors do worse at both ends.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Kristina Dahlin, Margaret Taylor & Mark Fichman, "Today's Edisons or weekend hobbyists: technical merit and success of inventions by independent inventors," *Research Policy* 33(8): 1167–1183 (2004), DOI 10.1016/j.respol.2004.06.003. Some records give the subtitle as "technical merits" or "technical impact." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048733304000848); [KCL Pure](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/todays-edisons-or-weekend-hobbyists-technical-merits-and-success-/); [HAL](https://shs.hal.science/hal-00480420/)
- **Evidence type:** a descriptive comparison of independent vs firm-based patents. Correlational. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048733304000848)
- **[Abstract, search-extracted] Question and measures:** do independent inventors produce the most or the least influential inventions in a product category? Independent and firm-based patents are compared on citation impact, detail, scope and maintenance. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048733304000848)
- **[Abstract, search-extracted] Sample and result:** 225 tennis-racket patents granted in the US, 1981–1991. Independent inventors are a heterogeneous group whose inventions are **over-represented among both the most impactful and the least impactful patents**. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048733304000848); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222838154_Today's_Edisons_or_Weekend_Hobbyists_Technical_Merit_and_Success_of_Inventions_by_Independent_Inventors); [HAL](https://shs.hal.science/hal-00480420/)
- **Corroboration from a follow-up's abstract:** "A study of the tennis racket industry by Dahlin et al. shows that independent inventors are a heterogeneous group which includes both 'heroes' who contribute substantially to technological progress and 'hobbyists' who make only a marginal contribution." — [Lettl, Rost & von Wartburg 2009, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048733308002965)
- **(secondary) Further reports:**
  - Singh & Fleming's working paper reads Dahlin et al. as showing that independent ("garage") inventors are over-represented in the tails of creative outcome distributions. — [INSEAD WP 2009/31](https://flora.insead.edu/fichiersti_wp/inseadwp2009/2009-31.pdf)
  - **Commercial success:** a 2018 Boston University IP Day slide deck cites Dahlin et al. (2004), alongside Åstebro, for the point that independent inventors' commercial success is low. — [Andersson & Tell slides](https://sites.bu.edu/tpri/files/2018/07/AnderssonTell_IPDay2018_BU.pdf)
  - **Earlier tennis-racket work:** Dahlin et al. (2000) reportedly found that independent inventors held fewer patents than corporate inventors in complex technologies. — [University of the Pacific paper](https://scholarlycommons.pacific.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1162&context=esob-facarticles)
- **Follow-up:** Lettl, Rost & von Wartburg (2009), "Why are some independent inventors 'heroes' and others 'hobbyists'? The moderating role of technological diversity and specialization," *Research Policy* 38(2): 243–254, DOI 10.1016/j.respol.2008.12.004. Per its title and abstract, it asks whether technological diversity and specialization explain the split between heroes and hobbyists. Its findings were not retrieved. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048733308002965); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/respol/v38y2009i2p243-254.html)

### Inferences
- **Reconciling Dahlin with Singh & Fleming.** Dahlin finds over-representation at both extremes; Singh & Fleming find lone inventors worse at both. Two differences may explain this:
  - Sample: 225 patents in one simple consumer-product category, vs 500,000+ patents across fields.
  - Definition: "independent" means not employed by a firm, which is not the same as working alone.

  A cautious reading: in simpler, more design-like product categories, independents can reach the top tail, but they also produce many duds. This is consistent with Chan et al. (2020) in Q6.
- **For the user:** expect solo/indie outcomes to be highly dispersed and skewed, as the indie Steam revenue data in `indie_game_market.md` also show. The useful question is what moves a solo creator from the "hobbyist" tail toward the "hero" tail. Lettl et al. studied exactly that (technological diversity vs specialization), but their answer was not retrieved.
- **Limitations (inferred):**
  - very small sample from a single industry;
  - patents measure technical, not commercial, merit;
  - descriptive and correlational.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: the exact tail proportions and the precise definition of "independent."
- **Unverified claim:** one search summary said a citing paper found *no significant median difference* in citation probability between independent and corporate patents. The citing source could not be identified.
- Lettl et al.'s (2009) findings on diversity vs specialization were not retrieved. They are worth checking, since they bear directly on the T-shape question for independent creators.

## Q5. Baker & Faulkner (1991): did combining roles (producer-director, writer-director) help or hurt film success?

### Takeaway
Only the abstract could be verified. In the blockbuster era, Hollywood **made the producer role more specialized**, separating business from art, while **fusing artistic roles**: the writer-director "hyphenate." Filmmakers adopted role combinations that solved technical and organizational problems (adaptation) and copied the role combinations of early blockbusters to gain legitimacy (imitation). **Whether combining roles raised box-office success could not be verified.** Three searches found no indexed statement of a performance result.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Wayne E. Baker & Robert R. Faulkner, "Role as Resource in the Hollywood Film Industry," *American Journal of Sociology* 97(2): 279–309 (1991), DOI 10.1086/229780. Faulkner's own publications page lists it as 1994; the journal record and Semantic Scholar give 1991. — [UChicago Press](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/229780); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Role-as-Resource-in-the-Hollywood-Film-Industry-Baker-Faulkner/f78a9d2c9af0ca582249118afe89a6d268a7f534); [Faulkner publications page](http://www.robertfaulkner.org/publications.html)
- **Evidence type:** a historical and sociological analysis of how roles changed. No verified performance test. — [UChicago Press](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/229780)
- **[Abstract] Concept:** a role is a resource in two senses:
  - a means to claim, bargain for, and gain membership and acceptance in a social community;
  - access to social, cultural and material capital that incumbents and claimants exploit to pursue their interests.
  - — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Role-as-Resource-in-the-Hollywood-Film-Industry-Baker-Faulkner/f78a9d2c9af0ca582249118afe89a6d268a7f534); [UChicago Press](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/229780)
- **[Abstract] Mechanisms:** "Through adaptation, filmmakers adopt role combinations with intrinsic capabilities of solving technical and organizational problems. Through imitation, filmmakers copy the role combinations associated with early blockbusters and gain legitimacy in Hollywood's institutional environment." — [UChicago Press](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/229780); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Role-as-Resource-in-the-Hollywood-Film-Industry-Baker-Faulkner/f78a9d2c9af0ca582249118afe89a6d268a7f534)
- **[Abstract] Trends:** "the increasing specialization of the producer and separation of the business and artistic domains, and the increasing fusion of artistic roles." — [UChicago Press](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/229780); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Role-as-Resource-in-the-Hollywood-Film-Industry-Baker-Faulkner/f78a9d2c9af0ca582249118afe89a6d268a7f534)
- **(secondary)** A 2020 UEA master's thesis, citing the paper, describes the period as the rise of the "artistic hyphenate":
  - business and artistic control were separated, and artistic roles such as director and screenwriter were consolidated;
  - 1980s producers relied more on proven themes and "blockbuster" potential.
  - — [UEA thesis](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/94587/1/ROSS%20MASTER%20THESIS%2015%20May%202020.%20Final.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The lesson is about *which* roles to merge.** The industry merged roles that need tight creative integration (writing plus directing) and split off a dissimilar domain (business). By analogy, a solo developer might fuse tightly coupled creative roles (design, art direction, animation) and get help with loosely coupled ones (business and marketing, possibly music or code). This is an analogy, not a tested result.
- **How widespread a practice is says little about how well it performs.** "Adaptation" implies functional benefits, but "imitation" implies some combinations spread for legitimacy rather than results. The spread of hyphenates alone does not show they performed better.

### Gaps
- **The performance effect is unverified.** Do **not** state that hyphenates earned more, or less, without the full text.
- Not retrieved: the sample (number of films, years) and the outcome measures.
- For verified film-industry evidence on depth vs breadth, use Mannucci & Yong (2018) on directors' careers; see `creative_innovation.md`.

## Q6. 2015–2026 studies that directly test T-shaped vs I-shaped expertise, or solo vs small-team creative output

### Takeaway
- **T vs I:** no study found directly compares T-shaped with I-shaped *individuals* on creative output.
- **Team composition:** the best mix of breadth and depth is **contingent on the technology** (Vakili & Kaplan 2021; partially verified for Melero & Palomeras 2015).
- **Solo vs team:** the most relevant new result is Chan, Mihm & Sosa (2020):
  - the lone-inventor penalty appears in utility (technology) patents but **not in design patents**;
  - it is smaller for **hard-to-decompose** inventions;
  - lone inventors gain more than teams from **past collaboration**.
- **Amateurs vs professionals:** Pollok et al. (2021) show hobbyist board-game teams beat professional teams at turning knowledge diversity into designs that are both novel and useful.
- **Games, apps, music:** no peer-reviewed study of solo vs team commercial outcomes was found.

### Cited Findings
**Chan, Mihm & Sosa (2020), "Revisiting the Role of Collaboration in Creating Breakthrough Inventions," *Manufacturing & Service Operations Management*, DOI 10.1287/msom.2019.0858.** Observational patent study; correlational.
- **Data:** utility and design patents, 1985–2009. — [INSEAD](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/revisiting-role-collaboration-creating-breakthrough); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2962348); [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/msom.2019.0858)
- **Utility vs design:** in utility patents, working alone is associated with lower odds of a breakthrough. In design patents that disadvantage does not appear, which the authors attribute to the "nearly nondecomposable nature of design." — [INSEAD](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/revisiting-role-collaboration-creating-breakthrough); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2962348)
- **Decomposability:** technology inventions that are hard to decompose "also relatively advantage lone inventors compared with teams," which the authors link to teams' higher coordination costs. — [INSEAD](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/revisiting-role-collaboration-creating-breakthrough); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2962348)
- **Past collaboration:** lone inventors benefit more than teams from having collaborated with others in the past. — [INSEAD](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/revisiting-role-collaboration-creating-breakthrough); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2962348)

### Inferences
- **Chan et al. is the most encouraging evidence for the user, with a condition.** Lone creators are *not* disadvantaged on design-type, tightly coupled ("nondecomposable") work, where splitting the work across people creates coordination costs.
  - A small 3D character-animation game is plausibly partly like this: character design, modeling, rigging and animation must cohere around one vision.
  - Other parts are more modular (engine and gameplay code, possibly music). That is where teams, contractors or AI help most.
  - This mapping is an inference; games were not studied.
- **Past collaboration is actionable (inference).** Since lone inventors benefit more from past collaboration, the user could collaborate on game jams, small contracts or co-projects before or during the solo project. That may supply the network and selection benefits Singh & Fleming identify.
- **Pollok et al. undercuts "only professionals/specialists succeed" in a game-design setting.** Amateurs turned diverse backgrounds into novelty more effectively than professionals. However, these were *teams* of hobbyists, not solo creators, and outcomes were novelty and usefulness, not sales.
- **The best configuration depends on context.** Breadth and depth trade-offs vary with technology type, uncertainty and modularity; there is no universal rule that specialists win. The T-shaped "skills" literature is mostly surveys with loose definitions and cannot settle T vs I for individuals.
- **The role of AI is speculative.** AI coding tools may act as a partial "team member" for the decomposable engineering work. They do not obviously supply the *selection* mechanism (outside critique) that Singh & Fleming emphasize. No study here tests this.

- **Not searched (budget):**
  - music (solo-written vs co-written hits);
  - film (writer-director/auteur box-office effects, 2015–2026);
  - apps (solo vs team app success);
  - GitHub team-structure studies.
- **Not verified:** Arts & Fleming (2018) and Kaplan & Vakili (2015), both still listed as unverified leads in `creative_innovation.md`.
- **Effect sizes not retrieved:** Chan et al. (beyond the single 17% snippet), Pollok et al., Vakili & Kaplan, and Melero & Palomeras.

## Q7. Cross-study answer: (1) can one person working across several roles produce successful creative work compared with teams? (2) does deep-plus-broad beat pure specialists and pure generalists?

### Takeaway
**(1) Solo work can succeed, but the average odds favor teams**, at least in patent-based evidence:
- Lone inventors produce fewer breakthroughs and more duds (Singh & Fleming).
- The penalty disappears for design work and is smaller for tightly coupled products (Chan et al.).
- The best-placed solo creators borrow team mechanisms: outside selection, diverse inputs, external networks and past collaboration.

**(2) Deep-plus-broad beats breadth alone and is associated with the highest value:**
- At 3M, only inventors with both breadth and depth were deemed highly valuable (Boh et al.).
- Peaks follow exploration *then* focused exploitation (Liu et al.).
- Whether deep-plus-broad beats pure specialists depends on context: specialists produce technically influential work, and the best configuration varies by technology (Vakili & Kaplan).

None of the evidence is causal.

### Inferences
- **On the user's belief that "people who do many things never go deep":** not supported as stated. Boh et al.'s polymaths combine breadth and depth, and Liu et al.'s peak performers explore broadly and then go deep. What *is* supported is narrower: breadth **without** depth gives volume but not influence (Boh et al.).
- **On "today only specialists succeed":** not supported as a blanket rule.
  - Commercial value at 3M required both breadth and depth.
  - Team-configuration studies find the answer depends on context.
  - Lone inventors are not disadvantaged in design work.

  The well-supported claim is different: **teams outperform lone individuals on average in technology** (Singh & Fleming). That is a statement about collaboration, not about specialization.
- **A plan consistent with the evidence (inference; untested for games):**
  1. Choose one deep leg tied to the game's core appeal (character animation) and go deep on it. Bring the other roles to "competent enough to integrate."
  2. Time-box an early exploration phase (art styles, mechanics), then commit and exploit (Liu et al.).
  3. Recreate the team's *selection* function with frequent outside critique and playtests. Recreate its *recombination* function with diverse inputs and an active creator network (Singh & Fleming's mediators; Chan et al.'s past-collaboration effect).
  4. Keep the tightly coupled creative core in one head (Chan et al.; Baker & Faulkner's fused artistic roles). Outsource, buy or AI-assist the modular parts: code, possibly music, business.
  5. Expect a skewed, widely dispersed outcome distribution (Dahlin et al.; the indie market data in `indie_game_market.md`).

### Gaps
- All the evidence is correlational. No experiment randomly assigns people to solo vs team work, or to T- vs I-shaped profiles, in creative production.
- None of the verified studies examines solo video-game development directly. None measures commercial game revenue against team size or skill profile.
- No study found tests whether AI tools close the gap between solo creators and teams.
